Case Study 01

Data Cafe: Designing the Backstage of AI

AI is only as good as the data you feed it. At EA, I designed a self-serve onboarding system that guides game teams to give their data meaning, so the AI tools built on top of it give answers people can trust.

Service DesignSystem DesignWorkflow AutomationAI Readiness
Role
Sole Experience Designer
Team
1 PM · 2 Data Eng
Timeline
6 Months · Ongoing
Platform
Web · Slack · Jira
The Challenge

Data Cafe is EA's data context platform, the source of truth for discovering, governing and using trusted data across every team and title. As game teams started plugging AI into their work, the AI tools at their disposal kept giving unreliable analysis. The models were fine. The trouble was messy, undocumented data with no definitions, no metadata and no shared language.

My Approach

My first thought was a cleaner onboarding screen. But a team building FC works nothing like a team building Battlefield, so beyond the UX of our data product, I designed the system behind it: a lightweight pipeline that runs inside the tools teams already use, so adding context happens during their normal day instead of as a separate chore.

01

Garbage In, Garbage Out: Finding the Real Problem

I started by listening. Across 71 user interviews and a survey of 24 people, one pattern kept showing up: demand for Data Cafe was high, but teams weren't ready. Nobody enjoys structuring data or writing context for it. It's tedious, and it takes forever. Meanwhile, every request to build a data catalog or onboard a new team lived in scattered Slack threads, long email chains and a lot of one-on-one handholding.

Data Producers
Data engineers and analysts who own the sources. They know their data best but have no time to document it, and every team catalogs differently.
Need: Publish Without the Busywork
Game Team Analysts & PMs
Consumers on titles like Battlefield, FC and Mobile who need fast, trusted answers, and increasingly, AI agents that can answer for them.
Need: Answers They Can Trust
The Platform Team
A small team handling every onboarding request by hand. Their white-glove support worked, but it couldn't scale past a few teams at a time.
Need: Scale Without Burning Out
"Provide data inventory, glossary/definitions, and searchable metadata so analysts can locate tables and metrics fast… enable users to ask agents, 'where did this metric come from?'"
CRM Engineering · Voice of the Customer

What changed the project for me was seeing that data readiness was the number one bottleneck, and that the teams that succeeded were the ones that got guided onboarding. So I focused on making that guidance self-serve rather than building another interface.

02

The Reframe: Designing the System Behind the Screen

I treated workflows and integrations as my design canvas. Each decision moved a manual, invisible step into a guided one that lives where teams already work.

Onboarding started with a Slack DM or an email to whoever you knew on the platform team.
One front door: an onboarding site with a single enrollment form for every team and every need.
Every team got the same conversation, whether they were brand new or ready to publish.
A readiness flow: four paths and two simple yes/no gates route each team to the right next step.
Teams were asked to hand-write descriptions for hundreds of tables and columns.
Automated enrichment: an n8n pipeline pulls context from Confluence, GitHub and Snowflake into Alation.
Requests got lost in threads, and nobody knew who was waiting on what.
Tracked by default: every enrollment becomes a Jira ticket and a Slack alert, with a reply within 2 business days.
03

Meeting Every Team Where They Are

Every team starts from a different place, so one checklist for everyone wouldn't work. I designed a conditional readiness flow where producers pick the journey that describes where they are today, answer a couple of simple questions and land on exactly one clear next step, whether that's publishing a data product or building an AI agent.

Path A · Fast Path
"I want to build with data that's already in Data Cafe."
  • Browse and request access to existing datasets
  • Create a data product
Path B · Bring Your Own Data
"I have my own data sources to catalog and publish."
  • Gate 1: Is your data cataloged in Alation? If not, enroll for onboarding.
  • Gate 2: Is it enriched with metadata? If not, run the n8n enrichment.
  • Create a data product
Path C · Build an AI Agent
"I want to create an AI agent using existing catalogs or data products."
  • Build, test and evaluate in Agent Studio
  • Publish an agent grounded in trusted Alation context
Path D · Guided
"I'm not sure where to start."
  • Submit one enrollment form
  • The platform team routes you to the right path
One front door for every producer and consumer, built in Figma Make.
Producers choose the journey that matches where they are today.
One signup form for every request, from onboarding to data product creation.
04

Building the Backstage Without an Engineering Cycle

I didn't want to wait for a roadmap slot, so I designed and built the pipeline myself with lightweight tools, then worked with our data engineers to harden it.

  1. Onboarding Site
    Built in Figma Make
  2. Enrollment Form
    One form for every need
  3. n8n Webhook
    Parses and routes requests
  4. Jira Ticket
    Auto-created and tracked
  5. Slack Alert
    Team is notified instantly
Figma Make: The Front Door
I wrote the site spec page by page and used Figma Make to turn it into a working onboarding site, with separate journeys for producers and consumers, the readiness flow and the enrollment form.
Claude Code: The Glue
I used Claude Code to write the form handlers, webhook payloads and scripts that connect the site to our internal tools, work that would normally have waited on an engineering sprint.
n8n: The Automation Layer
One workflow routes enrollments to Jira. Another enriches catalogs: it pulls context from Confluence, GitHub and Snowflake, generates descriptions and pushes them back to Alation. What used to be days of writing is now a 1 to 3 day run.
Jira & Slack: Where Teams Already Live
Nobody has to learn a new tool. The system shows up in the places people already check every day, and every request has a visible owner and gets an answer.
The n8n enrollment workflow: a webhook in, a Jira ticket out.
Every request becomes a tracked ticket.
The team is notified where they already work.
05

Outcomes & Reflections

447Total Platform Users+60.2% MoM
47Listed Data Products+9.3% MoM
174Active Agent Studio Users
From handholding to self-serve: Onboarding moved out of DMs and email threads into one tracked, repeatable pipeline that the platform team can scale.
Better input, better output: Teams that complete the flow publish data with definitions, ownership and metadata, the context AI tools need to give reliable answers.
Still growing: The work is ongoing, with more titles moving through the pipeline and agents next on the roadmap for Mobile and CRM.

Start With the Input: When AI output is bad, the instinct is to redesign the chat. On this project the leverage was backstage, in the experience of the people creating the data.

Workflows Are Design Work Too: The most useful thing I designed on Data Cafe was the path a request takes from a form to a ticket to a person.

Meet People Where They Already Work: Busy teams won't adopt a new tool to document their data. Bringing the process into Jira and Slack made good habits the easy default.

Case Study 02

EYQ: Enterprise Generative AI Platform

I led the design of a governed AI marketplace that gave consultants a secure, compliant way to use generative AI. It moved the firm off a sprawl of uncontrolled tools and onto one platform we could audit.

Experience StrategyPlatform DesignAI GovernanceB2B Enterprise
Role
UX Lead
Team
1 PM · 6 Eng · 2 Data Scientist
Goal
Build a governed AI marketplace.
Platform
Web Platform
The Challenge

Teams across the firm had started using external tools like ChatGPT on their own. The tools were powerful but risky: there was no governed way to standardize prompts, share workflows, or even track whether AI outputs were accurate or compliant with our data security policies. We needed to bring that work back under the firm's control.

Our Vision

We didn't want to ship another chatbot. We set out to build a centralized ecosystem where consultants could safely use pre-vetted "Skills," developers could publish reusable AI solutions, and admins kept complete visibility and compliance.

01

Mapping the Ecosystem

I started with research into how the firm actually worked. Three distinct personas emerged, and that made it clear this was a platform architecture problem as much as a design one. We needed to understand what drove each group and where they got stuck, then design flows that made sense to all three at once.

Client Practitioners
Consulting, Tax, and Assurance professionals who wanted AI help with daily tasks and faster workflows, using firm-approved resources without risking data security.
Goal: Streamline Workflows
EY Developers
Technical builders focused on creating and integrating AI solutions via low-code tools. They need clear governance processes for publishing skills and integrating with existing business systems.
Goal: Create Reusable Assets
Platform Admins
Managers responsible for tracking AI performance, monitoring user engagement, handling feedback, and ensuring strict data governance and compliance across global teams.
Goal: Monitor & Govern
02

Experience Strategy & Concepting

We defined core user journeys around selecting an existing skill, creating a custom skill, and monitoring usage.

System Architecture
Designed user flows allowing teams to build skills using contextual data, share with colleagues, and test outputs before publishing.
Low-Fidelity Concepting
Explored core interactions: the AI skill marketplace, prompt library management, and context-aware chat interactions.

Foundations: Wireframes & Systems Thinking

With three personas and competing needs, I mapped out the core workflows early using wireframes. At this stage I only wanted to check that the platform structure would actually work for all three user groups and that the information architecture made sense.

03

Designing for Adoption: The Practitioner Experience

Client Practitioners made up the bulk of our users, and they needed to feel confident right away. We designed an onboarding flow that made the idea of "Skills" concrete, with a welcoming tone and clear guidance. If practitioners didn't see the value on day one, adoption would stall.

The welcoming onboarding modal introducing the concept of a curated Skills library to users.
The main landing view, where users can switch between general 'EYQ Chat' and domain-specific 'Skills Chat'.
Detailed skill profile pages help users understand the specific use-case and accuracy rating before invoking the AI.
04

Core Interaction: Intelligent Guidance in the Chat

I designed the chat to guide people from the first screen, with suggestions tailored to the skill they'd chosen that they could use right away. A blank input field leaves people stuck, and I wanted them getting value faster without wasting their time.

The 'Skills Chat' welcome screen provides immediate, actionable prompt templates tailored to the selected domain.
Tooltips guide users on when to use standard AI versus expert, specialized skills.
An active chat session showing clear distinctions between the user's input and the AI assistant's response.
The input area stays in place, so users can keep refining their prompts.
05

Knowledge Sharing: A Prompt Library That Works

We made the shared prompt library a core platform feature. Consultants could build on what their colleagues had already worked out: if someone at the firm wrote a killer prompt, everyone else could use it right away.

The Prompt Management dashboard, allowing users to browse pre-tested queries by service line or sector.
06

For Developers: A No-Code Path to Publishing

The developer experience was tricky. Developers needed to build and publish AI Skills without wrestling with complex infrastructure. I designed a guided form flow that breaks prompt engineering, which gets complicated fast, into plain step-by-step workflows.

Step 1: Establishing global properties, targeted user groups, and specific service lines for the AI Skill.
Step 2: Connecting the skill to specific host URLs and defining core prompts before publishing.

I also wanted developers to see how their skills performed in real time, so I built out a detailed Skill Analytics dashboard with accuracy metrics, execution times, and direct user feedback as it came in. Creators could see what was working and keep improving.

The Skill Details view gives creators an immediate snapshot of accuracy and execution metrics.
The Analytics tab provides charts for Discovery Time and Response Time to monitor technical latency.
07

Closing the Loop: Admin Visibility & Governance

Admins couldn't govern what they couldn't see, so I built a dashboard that showed them the entire EYQ ecosystem at a glance: adoption rates, top-performing skills, and response times. When something went wrong, they needed to dig into the details, which is what the feedback interface was for.

The centralized Admin Dashboard tracking total active users, top 5 skills, and global response time metrics.

Admins needed to audit user feedback at scale. We built a dedicated feedback interface that made it easy to filter, review, and act on hundreds of user reviews across different countries. Admins no longer had to wait for escalations. They could deprecate failing skills and promote the high performers on their own.

No visibility into model performance or potential hallucinations in real-world use.
Full Audit Trail. Admins could review specific interactions, export data for compliance, and keep governance consistent across the firm.
Detailed review tracking, so admins can check that the AI stays compliant and helpful.
08

Outcomes & Reflections

78%Global Practitioner Adoption
50+AI Skills Created & Shared
5 minNew User Onboarding
Rapid Time-to-Value: Practitioners could start using AI skills within 5 minutes of signup, which made it much easier for people across the global firm to get started.
Network Effects: Over 50 AI skills were created within the first year, with practitioners freely sharing workflows across service lines and geographies.
Reduced Risk: With 78% adoption, the firm effectively stopped relying on ungoverned external AI tools and brought sensitive work back in-house.

Platform Thinking Over Feature Design: My biggest lesson was to stop thinking in "features" and think like a platform architect. Enterprise adoption depends on seeing the whole system and how Practitioners, Developers, and Admins rely on each other.

Balancing Speed With Governance: I had to hold the line on security and compliance without smothering innovation. Some of the best design decisions came from resisting the urge to over-engineer and instead asking: "What's the minimum governance needed?"

Make the Value Visible: Adoption spread fastest when teams could see real wins. Once the skill library was easy to find and the benefits of reuse were obvious, practitioners started recommending it to each other on their own.

Case Study 03

SaaS Contract Analyzer

I designed an AI-assisted contract audit tool that cut a 4-week manual grind down to 1 week. The machine learning stays in the background, and auditors make the calls.

AI/ML UXB2B SaaSFintechPrototyping
Role
UX Lead
Team
2 PMs, 2 Data Sci, 6 Devs
Goal
Audit Time: 4wks → 1wk
Platform
Web · Enterprise
The Reality

Our auditors were drowning in contracts, each one 300+ pages, and combing through them by hand ate weeks of time and thousands in unrealized billable hours every month. The firm already had the domain expertise, the historical annotations, and the data. What it didn't have was a system that could put them to work.

The Opportunity

We decided to pair machine learning with human judgment, so the model would help auditors instead of replacing them. If we could train a model on past annotations and flag likely problematic clauses, auditors could focus their expertise on validation and nuance. Our goal was to cut audit time from 4-5 weeks to 1-2 weeks and make the work less painful along the way.

Visualizing the manual contract analysis process.
01

Discovery: Bringing Everyone to the Table

I ran a workshop that got auditors, data scientists, engineers, and product leaders in the same room so we'd all start from the same understanding of the problem. One thing became clear quickly: we had to respect auditors' expertise and bring in ML as an assistant, not a threat.

Virtual design workshop defining problem statements and high-priority functions.
Human-in-the-Loop
Users needed the ability to confidently approve or reject machine-generated recommendations before they were finalized.
Scalable Architecture
The solution needed to be designed so it could eventually scale across different departments for various document types.
Manual Categorization
Even with ML, auditors required the fallback ability to manually add, highlight, and categorize clauses within the documents.
02

From Sketch to Reality: Rapid Iteration

After the workshop, I sketched out workflows based on the strongest stakeholder ideas, then we walked auditors through low-fidelity prototypes. That feedback was gold. It showed us where the flow broke down and what we'd misunderstood about their process.

Initial wireframing focusing on the interaction with machine-identified clauses.

Once the team aligned on approach, I moved quickly into high-fidelity using our in-house Motif design system. Two rounds of stakeholder testing later, we were hands-off with developers. I kept syncing with them weekly, though, because design problems almost always surface during the build.

Engagement page for contract setup.
Contract analysis view displaying annotated text.
03

The ML Reckoning: Handling Imperfection

The model identified financial sections flawlessly, which was a win. Categorization was messy, though. Most clauses got lumped into "other," which meant auditors still had to do the hard cognitive work we'd hoped to offload. We could either over-promise what the ML could do or be honest and design around its limits.

We chose honesty and designed a human-in-the-loop workflow around the model as it actually performed. Auditors would check its output, and their corrections would help it improve. To build confidence, I designed a power-user beta with six auditors over one month, plus dashboards so we could see exactly where the model was failing and iterate with the data science team.

Internal team dashboard for tracking model performance.
Detailed usage metrics for power users during the beta period.
04

Outcomes & Reflections

4w→1wAudit Time Reduced
92%ML Model Accuracy
80%Auditor Adoption
ML model accuracy was 80% in the first month and reached 92% after four months of company-wide launch.
Adopted by 80% of financial auditors across EY, accounting for roughly 50% of the revenue generated by the auditing department.

Bring Engineers Early: The best design decisions happened when I stopped designing in isolation and started designing with the engineering and ML teams. Knowing the feasibility constraints early gave me something real to design around, and the solutions got smarter for it.

Protect the Scope: I watched scope creep threaten the project multiple times. Pushing back on "nice-to-haves" and keeping the team focused on the core problem was some of the most important work I did.

Be Honest About the AI: The moment we stopped overselling the ML and started designing around its actual capabilities, adoption took off. Users respected that, and they felt like partners, not guinea pigs.

Case Study 04

Listing Doctor

I led the design of an error-tracking system that got Spreetail's quality team out of spreadsheet hell. Active listings went from 82% to 95% in three months.

UX ResearchPrototypingWorkshop FacilitationUsability TestingE-commerce
Role
UX Lead
Team
1 PM, 1 Tech Lead, 5 Devs
Goal
82% → 95% Active Listings
Platform
Web App · SaaS
The Bottleneck

Hundreds of products were broken across multiple marketplaces, and every day without fixes was costing Spreetail millions. The fixes themselves weren't hard. Finding the errors was, because the Excel dumps from each marketplace API arrived in a different format, so nobody could prioritize or even see how much was broken.

Our Approach

We set out to pull every error into one place, show what each one was costing, and let the quality team claim, track, and resolve work at their own pace. Our target: move from 82% active listings to 95% within the fiscal year.

Combing through error exports from different APIs, each in its own format.
01

Starting With a Workshop

I kicked things off with a hands-on workshop where people sketched their ideal day, mapped their workflows, and voted on solutions together. Marketplace Managers, Operations, Vendors, and the Quality Team each saw the problem differently, and pulling it all together pointed to three clear needs.

Affinity mapping and identifying high-priority problems.
Unified Overview Needed
Users wanted one clean overview of listing errors for each marketplace instead of raw Excel exports.
Financial Visibility
People wanted to see the direct financial impact of listings with unresolved errors so they could prioritize their workload.
Claiming & Noting
The quality team needed the ability to "claim" specific listing errors for resolution and attach notes for ongoing follow-ups.
02

Testing Wireframes Early

With insights fresh, I partnered with the PM to map user journeys and convert everything into low-fidelity wireframes. Then we tested immediately. The walkthroughs with real users let us iterate quickly, which we needed, because our first take on their mental model was close but not quite right.

Low-fidelity wireframe of the core layout.
Wireflow of how errors get categorized.

We ran another round of testing with these changes, and the reaction was immediate: users felt the tool had been built for them instead of working against them. Three months after we launched to the full team, we'd beaten our targets.

High-fidelity dashboard with error metrics.
03

What the Beta Users Asked For

We launched to eight beta users feeling confident, and they loved the interface: clear error messages, simple workflow. Then they asked to download error lists into Excel, which made me rethink the whole approach. My first instinct was to push back. When I listened instead, I saw how deeply their way of working was rooted in the tools they already used, and there was no point fighting that.

Revised layout incorporating search by item number.
Detailed error resolution panel.
Financial impact view for prioritization.
04

Outcomes & Reflections

$1.8MInventory Value Saved
90%Active Listings on Marketplaces
+8%Since Launch

After another round of user testing, we launched Listing Doctor to the entire user base. Within the first 3 months, the listing quality team saw an increase in their productivity and met their quarterly goals for resolved listing errors.

Reduce Friction Against Existing Mental Models: I learned the hard way that fighting entrenched workflows usually loses. So I added item-number search and export. They were small features, and they did a lot for adoption.

Make the Stakes Visible: Things changed when we added financial impact to every error. The work stopped feeling abstract because people could see exactly how much their effort mattered, and they got a lot more motivated.

Sometimes the Best Users Are Teachers: Beta users told us we were wrong, and the way they did it helped us build something better. Listening without getting defensive is one of the most useful things I can do as a designer.
